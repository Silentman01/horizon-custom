Every lesson from the newsletters & the Boron Letters

The Gary Halbert Playbook

He wrote the most mailed sales letter in history — 600 million copies. His ads generated billions. Here's everything he taught, explained so you can actually use it.

Gary C. Halbert (1938–2007) · "The Prince of Print"
↓
Lesson 01

Find a Starving Crowd

This is Halbert's most famous lesson. He'd ask his students: "If you and I both owned a hamburger stand and we were in a contest to sell the most burgers, what advantage would you want?"

Students would say better meat, lower prices, better location. Gary let them have ALL of those advantages. He only wanted one thing: a starving crowd.

Because it doesn't matter how good your burger is if nobody's hungry. And if people are STARVING, they'll buy a mediocre burger from a cart with no sign.

"A guy with a new product cannot always find a hot market for that product — but a guy who has uncovered a HOT MARKET can always find a product to fill the needs of that market." — Gary Halbert, The Boron Letters

This is the foundation of everything Gary taught. Market first. Product second. Always. Most entrepreneurs do it backwards — they build something and then go hunting for buyers. Gary said flip it: find a crowd of people desperately hungry for something, and THEN figure out what to feed them.

How to spot a starving crowd

Gary's method was dead simple: look at what people already buy. Not what they say they want. Not what surveys tell you. What they actually pull out their wallets and pay money for. That's real demand. Everything else is guesswork.

The hungriest markets are the ones where desire never dies — health, money, relationships, looking good, feeling important. These crowds have been starving for centuries and will keep starving forever.

Lesson 02

The A-Pile and the B-Pile

Gary said that when people get their mail, they instantly sort it into two piles without even thinking about it.

✓ The A-Pile — opened first

Personal-looking mail. Handwritten envelopes. A real stamp. Your name on it. A letter from a friend, a birthday card, something that looks like a real human sent it to you specifically.

✗ The B-Pile — trashed or ignored

Obvious junk. Bulk-printed labels. Metered postage. Window envelopes. "RESIDENT." Anything that screams "a company is trying to sell me something." Most of this goes straight in the trash.

You ALWAYS want to be in the A-Pile. This was the secret behind Gary's famous Coat of Arms letter — the most mailed sales letter in history. It looked like a personal letter from someone named Nancy Halbert, hand-addressed, with a real stamp. No logos. No company name. Nothing that whispered "advertisement."

This principle applies everywhere today. Your email shouldn't look like a corporate newsletter. Your ad shouldn't look like an ad. Your landing page shouldn't feel like every other landing page. Make it feel personal, human, and like it was sent to them specifically.

Lesson 03

The Only 3 Reasons People Don't Buy

Gary boiled it down to three and only three:

01
They don't want what you're selling
▼
You're pushing hamburgers on vegetarians. This is the hardest problem to fix — which is why Gary said to find the starving crowd FIRST. If you're solving this problem, you picked the wrong market. Back up and start over.
02
They can't afford it
▼
They want it, but the price is a barrier. This is fixable — payment plans, lower entry price, showing them the cost of NOT buying, reframing the price as an investment. But sometimes you're just selling to the wrong income bracket. Gary always targeted proven buyers — people who'd already demonstrated they had money AND willingness to spend it.
03
They don't trust you
▼
This is the easiest to fix — and the one Gary spent most of his career solving. They want it, they can afford it, but they don't believe YOU can deliver it. This is what great copy does: it builds trust through proof, stories, testimonials, guarantees, personality, and showing you understand their world. Almost every technique Gary taught was aimed at fixing this one problem.

When a sale doesn't happen, it's always one of these three. Gary said to diagnose which one it is and attack THAT problem specifically — don't just throw more words at the page hoping something sticks.

Lesson 04

The List Is Everything

In the direct marketing world, there's a classic rule called the 40/40/20 rule. It says your success breaks down like this:

40% — who you're selling to (the list/audience)
40% — what you're offering (the deal)
20% — how you say it (the copy)

Gary believed the list was even MORE important than 40%. He said it was closer to everything. You could write the worst ad in the world, but if you sent it to people who were already desperate to buy what you're selling, you'd still make money.

The golden rule of lists

The best list in the world is people who have already bought something similar to what you're selling, recently, and for a similar price. Not people who said they were "interested." Not people who signed up for a freebie. People who pulled out their wallet and paid real money. Buyers. Recent buyers. That's the list.

Gary called these "proven buyers" and he was obsessed with them. He'd rent mailing lists specifically filtered by recency (how recently they bought), frequency (how often they buy), and amount (how much they spend). This is why he always said to study what people DO buy — not what they say they might buy someday.

Lesson 05

The AIDA Formula

Someone once asked Gary if there was an update to the classic AIDA formula. His answer: "No. There is no update. I do not know of a better formula than that."

AIDA is the backbone of every piece of copy Gary ever wrote. It stands for:

A — Attention
Stop them in their tracks

They're busy. They don't care about you. You have one second to make them stop scrolling, stop flipping, stop walking past. This is your headline, your subject line, your first three words, your dollar bill stapled to the page. If you don't get attention, nothing else matters.

I — Interest
Make them want to keep reading

You got their attention — now hold it. Feed them interesting facts. Tell them something they didn't know. Promise them a secret. Make them curious. Every sentence should make them need to read the next sentence.

D — Desire
Make them want it badly

Now paint the picture. Use "word pictures" to let them see, feel, and taste what life looks like after they buy. Make the desire so vivid and urgent they start reaching for their wallet before you've even told them the price.

A — Action
Tell them exactly what to do

Don't be shy. Don't be subtle. Tell them to buy NOW. Tell them exactly how to order. Remove every possible obstacle. Make it so easy that NOT buying feels harder than buying. A confused buyer doesn't buy.

Lesson 06

Word Pictures

This is one of Gary's secret weapons that separates great copy from forgettable copy. A "word picture" is when you describe the benefit of your product so vividly that the reader experiences it in their imagination.

You don't just tell them what they'll get. You put them INSIDE the experience. You make them see it, feel it, hear it, smell it.

✗ Telling (boring)

"Our apartments have jacuzzis."

✓ Word picture (alive)

"Just wait until you step into that warm, inviting jacuzzi that comes with every apartment. The feel of that soothing, bubbling water against your bare skin is just plain heaven."

See the difference? The first one is a fact. The second one makes you FEEL it. Gary said your reader should be able to vicariously experience the wonderful benefits of owning your product just from reading your words. If they can already feel what it's like to own it, the sale is almost made.

Lesson 07

Headlines That Actually Work

Gary was blunt about this: if your headline doesn't work, nothing else matters. It doesn't matter how good your body copy is if nobody reads past the first line.

His rules for headlines were simple:

Put news in your headline. People are wired to pay attention to what's new. "New discovery," "just released," "for the first time" — these phrases trigger attention.
Promise a benefit. What's in it for them? Not what your product IS, but what it DOES for them. How does their life get better?
Do both at the same time. The best headlines combine news AND benefit. "New pill lets you eat whatever you want and still lose weight" hits both.
Be specific. "Make more money" is weak. "How to make $500 extra this weekend" is strong. Numbers, details, and specifics always beat vague promises.
Call out your reader. If your audience is dentists, say "Attention Dentists." If it's dog owners, say "dog owners." People stop when they see themselves being called out by name.

Gary also said to study the covers of Cosmopolitan and the National Enquirer — not for the content, but for the headlines. Those publications spend millions testing which headlines make people stop and grab the magazine. They're basically headline labs.

Lesson 08

The Coat of Arms Letter — A Masterclass in 381 Words

This is the most mailed sales letter in human history. 600+ million copies. It built a company that sold for roughly $90 million. And it was just one page long.

Here's what made it genius:

01
It looked like personal mail
▼
Plain envelope. Hand-addressed. Real stamp. No logo. No company name. It landed in the A-Pile every single time. People opened it because it looked like a letter from a real person — not a company trying to sell them something.
02
Hyper-personalized to THEIR name
▼
If your last name was MacDonald, the letter talked about the MacDonald family crest, the MacDonald coat of arms, the MacDonald family history. Your name appeared throughout. This wasn't "Dear Resident" — this was about YOU and YOUR family. It felt like someone did research specifically for you.
03
It came from "Nancy" — not a company
▼
Signed by Nancy Halbert (Gary's wife). A woman sharing a discovery she made while doing research for friends. She didn't sound like a salesperson. She sounded like a neighbor telling you about something cool she found. The trust was built in the very first sentence.
04
It sold identity, not a product
▼
Gary wasn't selling a piece of paper with a drawing on it. He was selling people a connection to their family history, a sense of heritage and pride. That's an emotional purchase, not a rational one. People bought it for what it made them FEEL — connected to something bigger than themselves.
05
Tiny price, zero friction
▼
The price was so low it was basically a no-brainer impulse buy. Easy to say yes, almost nothing to lose. This is the "tripwire" strategy — get a small purchase first, then sell them more later. Gary made the first "yes" as easy as possible.
Lesson 09

The Dollar Bill Trick (and the Principle Behind It)

Gary famously stapled real dollar bills to the top of his sales letters. The opening was always something like: "As you can see, I've attached a nice, crisp $1.00 to the top of this letter. Why have I done this?"

It worked for three reasons:

Attention. You physically cannot ignore a real dollar bill attached to a letter. Your brain won't let you. It interrupts everything and forces you to pay attention.

Reciprocity. Someone just gave you a dollar. Psychologically, you feel a tiny obligation to at least hear them out. You owe them a few seconds of your time.

Curiosity. "Why would someone mail me money?" You HAVE to read further to find out. And once you're reading, you're in Gary's world now.

The bigger principle

The dollar bill is just one example of a deeper idea: do something unexpected to break through the noise. In a world where everyone zigs, you zag. Attach something physical to your mail. Start your email with a shocking statement. Open your video with something nobody expects. The tactic changes — the principle never does.

Lesson 10

How Gary Actually Wrote Copy

Gary didn't just sit down and start writing. He had a process, and he drilled it into his students like a boot camp instructor:

Step 1: Research (Nugget Notes)

Before writing a single word of copy, Gary would gather everything — the product, competitor ads, books about the topic, magazine articles, anything relevant. Then he'd read through ALL of it and take what he called "nugget notes" — informal scribbles of any interesting fact, angle, phrase, or idea. No overthinking. Just capture anything that sparks.

Step 2: The dump

Now he'd sit at the keyboard and dump everything out. No editing. No polishing. Just pound out every thought, every angle, every claim, every story. Get it ALL out of your head and onto the page. This is not the time to be a perfectionist.

Step 3: The "So What" Test

Now he'd go back and read every single line with one brutal question: "So what?" If a line doesn't make the reader care, if it doesn't give them a reason to keep reading, if it doesn't move them closer to the sale — cut it or rewrite it. This test kills filler dead.

Step 4: Read it out loud

If it sounds weird when you say it, it'll feel weird when they read it. Copy should sound like a human talking to another human. If you stumble over a sentence out loud, your reader will stumble over it in their head.

Lesson 11

Write Like a Human, Not a Robot

Gary hammered this relentlessly: good writing is clear writing. Not clever writing. Not impressive writing. Clear writing.

His rules were brutally simple:

Use simple, everyday words. Write like you talk. If you wouldn't say "utilize" to your friend, don't write it in your ad. Say "use."
Keep sentences short. Short sentences are easy to read. They create rhythm. They feel punchy. Long, winding sentences lose people.
Keep paragraphs short too. Big blocks of text scare people away. Break things up. White space is your friend.
Use transitions. Words like "and," "but," "so," "here's the thing," "now listen" — these create flow. They're the grease that keeps the reader sliding down the page.
Write to ONE person. Not "customers" or "people." One person sitting across from you. Use "you" and "I." Make it feel like a conversation.
"A good writer makes things perfectly clear. A great writer makes complex things simple." — Gary Halbert
Lesson 12

Build a Swipe File (And Use It)

Gary said that every serious marketer needs a swipe file — a collection of ads, letters, emails, and promotions that worked. Not to copy. To STUDY. To understand what made them work. To have a library of proven patterns you can draw from.

His second piece of training advice was even more specific: hand-copy great ads. Literally sit down with a pen and write out famous sales letters word-for-word by hand. He said this imprints the rhythm, the structure, and the psychology of great copy into your brain in a way that just reading never does.

What to collect

Ads that made you stop scrolling. Emails you actually read all the way through. Subject lines you couldn't resist opening. Landing pages where you almost bought something. Headlines from magazines that grabbed you. Sales pages that made you pull out your wallet. Save everything that works on YOU — because it probably works on other people too.

Lesson 13

"Businesslike Personal"

Gary had a term for the ideal tone of a sales letter: "businesslike personal." It means your copy should feel like a letter from a real person who happens to have something valuable to offer — not like a corporate brochure, but also not like a text from your drinking buddy.

The personal part builds trust. You share stories. You use "I" and "you." You have a personality. You admit flaws. You sound human.

The business part builds credibility. You include professional-looking elements — maybe a typeset section, a photograph, a proper guarantee. You sound like someone who actually runs a real operation, not a scammer working out of a van.

The combo is the magic. Too personal and you seem unprofessional. Too business and you seem cold. Hit the sweet spot and people feel like they're getting an exclusive offer from someone they actually know and trust.

Lesson 14

Mind Follows Body

This isn't a marketing tactic. It's deeper than that. Gary believed that physical movement was the cure for creative block, mental fog, and emotional paralysis.

He told his son Bond to start every single day with an hour of "road work" — walking, jogging, running. Not to get in shape (though that was a bonus). Because how you feel physically controls how you think.

"When you get stuck or emotionally jammed up, one of the ways to get yourself unclogged and flowing again is just to keep moving. Run. Walk. Jog. Write. Do the dishes. But don't sit around waiting for a flash from Heaven." — Gary Halbert, The Boron Letters

Gary wrote his best copy after morning exercise. He believed you couldn't produce great work from a sluggish body. And his principle for writer's block was the same: the key is movement. If you can't write the perfect thing, write ANYTHING. Momentum creates quality. Sitting still creates nothing.

Lesson 15

Make Saying "Yes" Stupidly Easy

Gary pointed out that many great businesses died simply because a competitor made it slightly easier to buy from them. Great burger stands went under when the place across the street added a drive-thru. Same food. Just easier to get.

His rules for the close:

Reduce price options. Don't give them 15 choices. Give them 2 or 3 at most. Too many options = paralysis = no sale.
Remove risk. Guarantee everything. "If you don't love it, I'll give you every penny back." The stronger and longer the guarantee, the MORE people buy — and the FEWER people return. It's counterintuitive but it works every time.
Tell them exactly what to do. "Click the green button below." "Call this number now." "Fill out the card and drop it in the mail." Never assume they know the next step.
Add urgency — but make it real. Limited supply. Price going up. Deadline approaching. But it has to be genuine, or you destroy trust permanently.
Lesson 16

Study What They Buy, Not What They Say

Gary was obsessed with observable behavior over stated intentions. People lie in surveys. People lie to themselves. But their credit card statements don't lie.

He taught three filters for evaluating any market or list:

Recency — How recently did they buy? Someone who bought something last week is 10x more likely to buy again than someone who bought six months ago. Recency is the strongest predictor of future buying.

Frequency — How often do they buy? Repeat buyers are gold. They've proven they're not one-time impulse purchasers — they have an ongoing appetite.

Monetary value — How much do they spend? Someone who spends $200 on supplements is a different animal than someone who only buys when it's $5 or less.

Gary said these three factors — Recency, Frequency, and Monetary value (RFM) — tell you more about a customer than any demographic profile ever could. A 25-year-old who buys golf gear monthly and a 65-year-old who does the same? Same customer to you.

Putting It All Together

The Halbert Method — Start to Finish

If Gary Halbert were looking over your shoulder right now, here's the exact order he'd tell you to do things:

Your step-by-step process

Find the starving crowd. Look at what people are already buying. Find a market with urgent, recurring demand. Don't invent a product first — find the hunger first.
Get the best list you can. Target proven buyers — recent, frequent, big spenders. The list is more important than the copy. A great letter to the wrong list is a waste of paper.
Find or create the right offer. Something that satisfies what this crowd is hungry for. Low risk, high perceived value. Make the first "yes" easy and cheap if you can.
Research like a maniac. Gather everything — competitor ads, books, articles, testimonials, data. Take nugget notes on anything that sparks interest. Build a mountain of raw material.
Get in the A-Pile. Whether it's email, mail, or ads — make it look and feel personal. Don't look like every other ad. Look like a message from a real human to a real human.
Follow AIDA. Grab attention (headline/hook). Build interest (fascinating facts and stories). Create desire (word pictures showing the result). Drive action (tell them exactly what to do and make it easy).
Write to ONE person. Simple words. Short sentences. Short paragraphs. Transitions. Make it flow. Read it out loud. Apply the "So What" test to every line. Kill the filler.
Build trust. Use proof, stories, specifics, guarantees, and a real human personality. Sound businesslike-personal. Remove every reason not to trust you.
Make buying stupid easy. Few choices. Strong guarantee. Clear instructions. Genuine urgency. Remove every possible point of friction.
Test, measure, repeat. Gary tracked every dollar. Every list. Every headline. Don't guess — test. Let the numbers tell you what works. Then do more of that.
