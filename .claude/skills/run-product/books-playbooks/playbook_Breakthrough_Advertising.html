Chapter 1

Mass Desire: The Force That Makes Advertising Work

Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product.

Core Law

The Amplification Effect

When advertising exploits already-existing desire, $1 spent can create $50 or even $100 in sales. When it tries to create desire, it becomes education — producing at best $1 in sales per $1 spent. No single advertiser can afford to educate the public. You must ride forces far greater than any ad budget.

Two Categories of Mass Desire

1. Permanent Forces

Mass Instinct: Women wanting to be attractive, men wanting virility, everyone wanting health. The desire never fades. Your problem: distinguish your product from competitors — create a fresh appeal, stronger believability.

Mass Technological Problem: Bad TV reception, corroding mufflers, slow aspirin. Until solved, customers buy and try. Your problem: offer the same claim in a new way.

2. Forces of Change

Trends: Style shifts (swimming pools replacing bigger cars). Need sensitivity, foresight, intuition. Catch the rising tide when it's barely perceptible.

Mass Education: Tastes trickling down from society figures, TV stars, community pressure. When does the shift become statistically significant?

The 3-Step Channeling Process

STEP 1Choose the most powerful desire that can be applied to your product. Evaluate by: urgency, staying power, scope.
→
STEP 2Acknowledge that desire in a single headline statement. Meet your prospect at his current point of awareness.
→
STEP 3Show how your product's performances inevitably satisfy that desire throughout the body copy.

The Three Dimensions of Desire

  1. Urgency / Intensity — Degree of demand to be satisfied. (Arthritic pain vs. minor headache.)
  2. Staying Power — Degree of repetition, inability to become satiated. (Raw hunger vs. gourmet craving.)
  3. Scope — Number of people who share this desire. (Men wanting to save gas vs. prevent repair bills.)

Physical Product vs. Functional Product

Every product is actually two products. The physical product (steel, glass, tobacco) does not sell. People buy the functional product — what it does. The physical product only:

• Justifies your price  • Documents quality of performance  • Assures continued performance  • Sharpens the reader's mental picture  • Gives a fresh basis for believability

Your first task: list every performance your product contains → group them against the mass desires each satisfies → feature the ONE that harnesses the greatest sales power at this particular time.

Chapter 2

Your Prospect's State of Awareness

Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad.

Critical Rule

A headline that works for a market in one stage of awareness will NOT work for a market in another stage. Nor will it work even to a market in which it has been successful, once that market passes on to a new stage.

The 5 Stages of Awareness

StageProspect Knows...Headline StrategyExample
1Your product, what it does, and wants it. Just hasn't bought yet.Product name + bargain price. Nothing creative needed."Revere Zomar Lens — Formerly $149.50 — Now $119.95"
2Product exists but doesn't yet want it — not fully convinced.Display name/logo + point out superiority. 7 possible tasks: reinforce desire, sharpen image, extend image, new proof, new mechanism, eliminate limitations, change image."At 60 mph, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock"
3Wants what the product does, but doesn't know your product exists.Name the desire and/or solution in headline. Prove it can be done. Show mechanism is in your product."How to Win Friends and Influence People"
4Has a need but doesn't realize the connection to your product.Name the need/solution. Dramatize the need. Present product as inevitable solution."Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery"
5Not aware of desire or need, or won't admit it. Most difficult.NO price, NO product name, NO direct claim. Lead with identification — echo an emotion/attitude. Sell the rest of the ad, not the product."They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano — But When I Started to Play!"

Stage 5 Headline Strategies

When all direct methods fail, you must write an identification headline. You are calling your market together — telling them what they are — defining them for themselves. Rules:

  1. Eliminate all mention of price
  2. Keep your product out of the headline
  3. No direct statement of performance, desire, or problem
  4. The prospect must IDENTIFY with your headline before he can buy from it
  5. Build a steady progression: identification → awareness of problem → realization of solution → focus on your product
8 Stage-5 Pattern Examples from Schwartz

1. Giving Words to a Hidden Dream: "The University of the Night" — expanded market for home correspondence courses beyond "earn more money" headlines.

2. Exploiting a Hidden Fear: "Why Men Crack..." — revitalized a dying coffee substitute brand (Postum).

3. Leading Into an Unacceptable Problem: "Within the Curve of a Woman's Arm" — launched women's deodorant when the topic was unpublishable.

4. Projecting a Hidden Desire: The Marlboro Tattoo Ad — projected virility through visuals when words would be rejected as absurd.

5. Using Common Resentment: "Why Haven't TV Owners Been Told These Facts" — broadened market beyond handymen by exploiting resentment against repair bills.

6. Projecting Ultimate Triumph: "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" — sold music lessons via identification with triumph.

7. Projecting Result of a Problem: "Often a Bridesmaid But Never a Bride" — sold mouthwash by projecting the result of bad breath rather than naming it directly.

8. Projecting Result of Accomplishment: "Here's an Extra $50, Grace—" — sold correspondence courses by showing reward, not effort.

"Markets change; desires change; fashions change. And so do the acceptable styles of advertisements change. When a new style is born, people believe it. When that same style grows trite, people cannot see the message for the advertisement." — Eugene Schwartz
Chapter 3

Market Sophistication: How Many Products Have Been There Before You?

How many similar products have been presented to your market before yours? This determines how you must compete.

The 5 Stages of Sophistication

StageMarket StateYour StrategyHeadline Approach
1You are first. Prospects have zero previous info.Be simple. Be direct. Don't be fancy. State the need or claim — nothing more."Now! Lose Ugly Fat!"
2A competitor's direct claim is working. You're second.Copy the successful claim but ENLARGE it. Drive it to the absolute limit. Outbid."Lose Up to 47 Pounds in 4 Weeks — Or Receive $40 Back!"
3Prospects have heard all the claims in all their extremes. Skepticism rises.Introduce a NEW MECHANISM. Shift emphasis from WHAT it does to HOW it works. The mechanism goes in the headline; the claim is compressed."Floats Fat Right Out of Your Body!"
4Competitors have copied your mechanism. Elaboration war begins.Elaborate/enlarge the mechanism. Make it easier, quicker, surer."First NO-DIET Reducing Wonder Drug!"
5Market is exhausted. Nobody believes any ad. Competitors dropping out.Abandon promise & mechanism. Shift to IDENTIFICATION with the prospect himself. (Same strategy as Stage 5 Awareness.)"Why Men Crack..." / Marlboro Man
Warning

The Disintegration Pattern: As Stage 2 runs its course — the successful claim is overworked, enlargement piles on enlargement, new competitors each promise more, words lose meaning ("whiter-than-whites"), believability shatters, the government investigates, and the sales curve turns down.

Case Study: The Cigarette Industry Through All 5 Stages

Stage 1: "I'd Walk a Mile for a Camel!" — raw promise of enjoyment.

Stage 2: "Light Up a Lucky, and You Won't Miss the Sweets That Make You Fat!" — enlarged comparison.

Stage 3: "Luckies — They're Toasted!" / "Pall Mall's Greater Length Filters the Smoke Further!" — new mechanisms.

Stage 4: "Philip Morris — All the Harshness Baked Out!" — mechanism elaboration.

Stage 5: Marlboro "Virile Men" ads — pure visual identification, no claims, no headlines.

The filter sub-market then re-traced all 5 stages again: Kent → Viceroy → Parliament → Tareyton → back to "Winston Tastes Good Like a Cigarette Should!"

Chapter 4

38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline

Verbalization: the art of increasing headline impact by how you state the claim. You know WHAT to say — now determine HOW to say it.

Verbalization can: (1) Strengthen the claim — enlarge, measure, make more vivid. (2) Make it new again — twist it, change angle, turn into narration. (3) Pull the prospect in — promise information, question him, partially reveal mechanism.

  1. Measure the SIZE — "20,000 Filter Traps in Viceroy!"
  2. Measure the SPEED — "In Two Seconds, Bayer Aspirin Begins to Dissolve in Your Glass!"
  3. COMPARE the claim — "Six Times Whiter Washes!"
  4. METAPHORIZE the claim — "Melts Away Ugly Fat!"
  5. SENSITIZE it (feel/smell/touch/see/hear) — "The Skin You Love to Touch!"
  6. DEMONSTRATE via prime example — "At 60 mph, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock"
  7. DRAMATIZE the claim or result — "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano"
  8. State as PARADOX — "How a Bald-Headed Barber Saved My Hair!"
  9. REMOVE LIMITATIONS — "Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery!"
  10. ASSOCIATE with aspirational values/people — "9 Out of 10 Decorators Use Wundaweave"
  11. Show how much WORK the claim does — "Relief From All 5 Acid-Caused Stomach Troubles—In Seconds!"
  12. State as QUESTION — "Who Else Wants a Whiter Wash—With No Hard Work?"
  13. Offer INFORMATION — "How to Win Friends and Influence People!"
  14. Tie AUTHORITY into claim — "Here's What Doctors Do When They Feel Rotten!"
  15. BEFORE-AND-AFTER — Coldene: 5 days of suffering → 5 days, done.
  16. Stress NEWNESS — "Now! Chrome Plate Without Heat, Electricity, Machinery!"
  17. Stress EXCLUSIVITY — "Only Gleem Has GL-70!"
  18. Turn into CHALLENGE — "Which Twin Has the Toni?"
  19. CASE-HISTORY QUOTATION — "Look, Mom — No Cavities!"
  20. CONDENSE — interchange product and replacement — "Now! A Ring and Piston Job in a Tube!"
  21. SYMBOLIZE — parallel reality — "Starting Next Tuesday, the Atlantic Ocean Becomes Only One-Fifth as Long!"
  22. Connect MECHANISM to claim — "Floats Fat Right Out of Your Body!"
  23. CONTRADICT expected mechanism — "Hit Hell Out of the Ball With Your Right Hand!"
  24. Connect NEED and claim — "There Is Only One Solution to an Advertising Problem: Find the Man!"
  25. Offer INFORMATION in the ad — "Why Men Crack..."
  26. Turn into CASE HISTORY — "Again She Orders — A Chicken Salad, Please"
  27. NAME the problem — "When You're Weary with Day-Time Fatigue..."
  28. WARN about pitfalls — "Don't Invest One Cent Until You Check This Guide!"
  29. EMPHASIZE via phraseology — "Nobody But Nobody Undersells Gimbel's!"
  30. Show EASY via overcome-limitation — "If You Can Count to Eleven, You Can Increase Your Speed at Numbers!"
  31. State the DIFFERENCE — "The Difference in Premium Gasolines Is in the Additives!"
  32. SURPRISE with overcome limitations — "See What Happens When You Crush a Hartman DC-8? Nothing!"
  33. Address people who CAN'T buy — "If You've Already Taken Your Vacation, Don't Read This."
  34. ADDRESS prospect directly — "To the Man Who Will Settle for Nothing Less Than the Presidency"
  35. Dramatize HOW HARD to produce — "When Jens Finished Designing This Candleholder We Had to Invent a Whole New Kind of Candle"
  36. Accuse claim of being TOO GOOD — "Is It Immoral to Make Money This Easily?"
  37. CHALLENGE limiting beliefs — "You Are Twice as Smart as You Think"
  38. Turn into QUESTION AND ANSWER — "You Don't Know What's Under the Hood... Who Should You See? Someone Who Cares — United Delco."
Chapter 5

The Art of Creative Planning

What you are looking for in this product and this market is the one element that makes them unique. No outside formula will give it to you.

The 3 Levels of Creativity

Level 1 — Weakest

Word-Substitute

Swap your product name into a proven headline. Creates "Echo Ads" — reminding people of some other product. Pays no attention to the unique product-market-timing relationship.

Level 2 — Better

Formula

Memorize rules/principles, pour your headline in like hot lead into a mold. Valid for verbalization techniques. But the IDEA for the ad demands its own shape.

Level 3 — Best

Analysis

No answers — only guideposts and questions. The solution which cost days of painful effort can be used only once. Every new product-market-timing relationship is unique. The solution is equally unique. Copying — even copying yourself — leads to file-cabinet mediocrity.

Special Planning Insights

On Motivation Research

MR findings are directions, not headlines. They tell you where NOT to go, then indicate the general area of your solution. The transformation of facts into an idea, and expression in the strongest form, still requires creative talent. MR is also invaluable for testing your own hunches — answering the "why" behind failures and roadblocks.

On Product Personality

Every product has a distinct, complete personality to the consumer. One trait will always be most effective in summarizing that personality (e.g., Cadillac = quality). Feature that dominant trait in headlines, then expand the full personality in body copy — an ever-enlarging pyramid of persuasion.

On Prevention Headlines

A man will NOT visualize future disasters occurring to HIMSELF — but he IS capable of visualizing them affecting others around him. That's why cavity-preventing toothpaste sells when the ad focuses on the children, and life insurance sells by picturing the family left behind.

On Splinter Markets

A small company can avoid competing for the core market and concentrate on a smaller segment. Sociology-based segmentation (e.g., health-focused dieters vs. appearance-focused) is often more effective than geography. If successful, use that success as a point-of-difference when invading the majority market later.

Chapter 6

Inside Your Prospect's Mind

From the headline on, your body copy does the selling. It does this by altering your prospect's vision of reality — creating a new world where your product emerges as the fulfillment of his dominant desire.

The 3 Dimensions of Your Prospect's Mind

1. Desires

Wants, needs, cravings. Physical, material, sensual. You cannot create them. But you can expand, sharpen, channel, and give them a goal. Your primary task: make your prospect WANT. Picture every moment of fulfillment.

2. Identifications

Roles your prospect wants to play. Personality traits he wants to project. Not material or sensual — they complement desires by making each purchase serve double duty. Goals, hopes, dreams, ambitions, envies — never openly spoken.

3. Beliefs

Opinions, attitudes, prejudices, conceptions of reality. The emotionalized reason he inhabits. May be shallow or profound, valid or false. Advertising cannot argue with them or change them. You must accept reality and exploit its tendencies — building conviction using HIS kind of logic, not yours.

These three dimensions determine: how long your ad must be (enough to build desire, make him comfortable, make him believe) — its structure — its development, style, and pace.

Chapter 7 — Technique 1

Intensification: 13 Ways to Strengthen Desire

Advertising is the literature of desire. It is society's encyclopedia of dreams. Your job: fill out vague desires with concrete images — show every possible way they can be fulfilled.

Rule

You cannot repeat, but you can reinforce. Every time the same basic promise is given a fresh setting, it reinforces what went before and makes your prospect more determined to participate.

  1. Present claims directly — thorough, completely detailed description of appearance or results. Don't hold back.
  2. Put the product in action — show not just what it looks like, but exactly HOW it works in step-by-step motion.
  3. Bring in the reader — put him smack in the middle. "Picture this: next weekend you go down to your car..."
  4. Show him how to test your claims — let him visualize proving the performance himself.
  5. Stretch benefits in time — show the product at work over weeks and months, a continuous flow of benefits.
  6. Bring in an audience — celebrities (testimonials), average people (case histories), friends, family reactions.
  7. Show experts approving — nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts. Surprise + competition + discovery.
  8. Compare, contrast, prove superiority — lay disadvantages of old product side by side with advantages of new.
  9. Picture the black side too — irritate the wound, then apply the salve. Two currents: repulsion FROM old + attraction TO new.
  10. Show how easy it is — stress ease of application, contrast with tremendous benefits received.
  11. Use metaphor, analogy, imagination — present facts in more dramatic form. ("Suppose he himself were standing at your elbow...")
  12. Summarize: the catalog — condensed listing of all performances/benefits/applications. Two types: horizontal (wider applications) and vertical (deeper desire).
  13. Put your guarantee to work — turn it into the CLIMAX of your ad. Restate performances reinforced by guarantee at every step.

Intensification in the Campaign

The problem shifts from building desire throughout one ad to building desire throughout a series. You need:

1. A Central Campaign Idea compressed into a single statement or picture so powerful it sells the first time AND continues to sell on repetition.

2. A series of variations/perspectives — each emerging from the central idea but so different they compel re-reading and make it seem new again.

Campaign techniques range from: precise slogan repetition with changing illustrations (Toni, Clairol) → underlying theme only with all-new ads each time (VW: "Think Small", "Lemon", "Our new model") → the constant image anchor (Cadillac quality).

Chapter 8 — Technique 2

Identification: Building a Saleable Personality Into Your Product

There is another kind of desire — far more subtle, partly unconscious, longing not for satisfaction but for expression. The Longing for Identification.

Two Types of Roles

Character Roles

Expressed by adjectives: "progressive," "chic," "brilliant," "well-read." Once mastered, they must be ACKNOWLEDGED — or they are worthless. Products serve three ways: help achieve mastery, simplify/speed mastery, or symbolize mastery to invoke admiration.

Because character roles are ambiguous, untested, partly subconscious — prospects are far MORE ready to believe them than performance claims. Acceptance is easy, painless, non-demanding.

Achievement Roles

Status/position roles expressed by nouns as titles: "Executive," "Home Owner," "$20K-a-Year Man," "Career Woman," "Good Mother." Each must be won, held, and DISPLAYED.

Products become physical symbols of these invisible achievements. We are known not only by the company we keep — but by the products we own.

How to Build Identification Into Your Product

Step 1: Find the Primary Image. What personality does the product already have in your prospect's mind? (A cigarette = virile. A piston ring = precision-machined. Household appliances = time-saving.)

Step 2: Intensify or subordinate the primary image. If favorable, dramatize it (Marlboro = virility + tattoo + situation + man). If negative or neutral, retain it but lower its intensity — use it as a bridge.

Step 3: Graft on new, favorable images. Use the accepted primary image as a logical link to bring in additional compelling images (success, adventure, romance, culture). These must emerge from the primary image and be consistent with its broadest definition.

Critical Warning

The Believability Chasm

If you demand your prospect jump across a believability chasm, your ad will fail. (E.g., an elite society image to sell mass-market deodorant soap.) If the prospect can't identify himself with the image — if it contradicts his present position in life — disbelief spreads from the identification to the performance claims themselves, killing sales.

Solution: Always build a bridge of ideas or images — starting on HIS side. First insert an image he can immediately identify with, then use that as a hook for your target image.

Identification From the Physical Product

Three areas of image potential: (1) Appearance — borrow from extreme forms (racing car streamlining → family sedan). (2) Components & Structure — show radar, guided missiles that use the same parts. (3) Technical Background — Johnson & Johnson's Micrin bottle mimicked doctor's office glass containers = instant "this is medicine, this works."

Chapter 9 — Technique 3

Gradualization: Making Prospects Believe Before You Claim

One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises.

Core Principle

Every claim has TWO sources of strength: (1) the content itself, and (2) the preparation that has been made for it — either already existing in the prospect's mind, or deliberately built in the preceding copy. You can strengthen any statement by changing its POSITION in the ad.

The Architecture of Belief

Gradualization = starting your ad with a statement that will be immediately and entirely accepted, then building a chain of subsequent acceptances. The purpose: lead the reader to a goal conclusion which he would not have accepted without the preliminary statements.

Belief-Building Devices

  1. The Inclusion Question — "Was your set purchased after 1947?" Forces immediate identification. Builds a Yes-train.
  2. Detailed Identification — Catalog symptoms/problems the prospect is experiencing. Every accurate detail = another agreement. "He's talking about ME."
  3. Contradiction of Present (False) Beliefs — "Forget everything you've heard about what age must do to your appearance." Loosens ingrained limitations. Must be backed by strong authority.
  4. The Language of Logic — "The reason for this is clear..." / "Therefore..." / "Here's why..." / "This has been proved by thousands..." Words that carry the FLAVOR of conviction regardless of content.
  5. Syllogistic Thinking — Formal logical structure where one statement inevitably leads into another. Creates feeling of INEVITABILITY. "Larger spark → more powerful explosion → more power from gasoline."
  6. Contingency Structures — "If... then..." / "Was your... then..."
  7. Repetition of Proof: Echoing — "These experts found... These experts found... These experts found..."
  8. Promise-Belief-Promise Variation — Every sentence of promise followed by one of proof, so the reader never has breathing space to question.
  9. Paragraph Parallelism — Use the same word structure from an accepted statement to borrow acceptance for a fresh claim.
"If you violate your prospect's established beliefs in the slightest degree, nothing you promise, no matter how appealing, can save your ad. But if you can channel the tremendous force of his belief behind only one claim, that one fully-believed claim will sell more goods than all the half-questioned promises your competitors can write." — Eugene Schwartz
Chapter 10 — Technique 4

Redefinition: Removing Objections to Your Product

Redefinition says the product is THIS rather than THAT. Its objective: remove a roadblock to the sale — ideally before the prospect even knows it exists.

Three Types of Redefinition

1. Simplification

For products that sound too complicated or hard to use. Don't just scream "EASY!" — redefine the entire activity. The TV repair book ad never mentioned "repair." It redefined repairs as "adjustments," troubles as "warning signals," and showed that the task was no harder than taking a cold tablet.

The Sherwin Cody ad redefined English mastery: only 69 words make up half of all speech; only 12 principles of punctuation; only 25 typical grammar errors. When study is made so simple, 15 minutes a day is enough.

2. Escalation

For products that aren't important enough — whose appeal is too narrow. Broaden the horizon of benefits. Redefine the role the product plays in the prospect's life. Show it enters dozens of vital situations every day.

Escalate importance: "You pay $4,000 for your car. And a single 99¢ part robs you of the real power."

Escalate frequency: "Your Entire Life is Spent Trying to Get Others to Do What You Want."

Escalate benefits: Vocabulary → not just good English, but the power to hold people spellbound, win them to your point of view.

3. Price Reduction

For products that cost too much. Switch the comparison standard. The spark plug ad at $1.49 each (1.5× standard) first established: hand-made quality → "would sell for $5 each" → "$30-40 for a set" → "price down so low" → "pay for themselves 12 times" → condition of testing → "prepared to do" → "you can HAVE" → "only $1.49."

Ten separate bargain-phrases in two paragraphs — most never consciously noticed by the prospect. He simply feels a bargain is being offered.

The Flip-Flop: The simplest and often most effective redefinition. A complete reversal — turning a liability into an asset with a single idea. Lifebuoy's horrible medicinal odor became proof of odor-destroying power. Wherever you can use this, do so.

Chapter 11 — Technique 5

Mechanization: Verbally Proving Your Product Works

"How does it work?" Your prospect wants a mechanism — a logical demonstration of HOW your product delivers its promise.

Three Stages of Mechanism

StageWhen to UseWhat to Do
1Prospect already understands how the product works.Name the mechanism. "Electronic Light Setter... Push-Button Zomar Lens... Magazine Load." Don't describe — just name in bold type and compete on price/features.
2Mechanism is not well known — prospect needs convincing.Describe the mechanism in selling language. Promise → Reason Why. Load every word with emotion and desire. The mechanism copy must SELL just as hard as the promise copy. (Rinso: "Dirt floats off — stains go.")
3Market is highly sophisticated. Promises sound alike. Need differentiation.Feature the mechanism in the headline itself. The mechanism BECOMES the main claim. "Floats Fat Right Out of Your Body" / "First Wonder Drug for Reducing." Offers a NEW WAY — a fresh chance for success even if everything else has failed.

Mechanism for Price Cuts

Price cuts must be JUSTIFIED. Without a mechanism — a reason-why you should give this bargain — you get only a fraction of its real sales power. Robert Collier mastered this: the unseasonable weather caused the factory to become overstocked, resulting in the markdown. He started with the mechanism and only revealed the bargain six paragraphs later — reaping ten times the believability.

Chapter 12 — Technique 6

Concentration: Destroying Alternate Solutions

Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time.

The 5 Weapons Against Competition

1. Superiority of product — the ultimate weapon. 2. Superiority of promise — stronger, wider, more believable. 3. Product-role — personality, prestige, excitement. 4. Response & reaction — ability to escalate, shift, invade new markets. 5. Direct attack — Concentration.

Two Concentration Structures

Structure A: Point-by-Point Interweaving

Bad → Good → Bad → Good → Bad → Good.

A weakness in the competitor is pointed out, then immediately counteracted by your product's benefit. Repeat for each performance factor. Uses parallelism, redefinition, contrast of image ("thin skimpy spark" vs. "heavy powerful flame").

Structure B: Time-Sequence

What happens to you NOW with old products → What will happen when you switch to the new.

Sharpen the unpleasant recurring experience the prospect knows too well (building agreements), then provide the antidote. The reducing pill ad: passive plans → will power → failure → fat returns → THEN the new pill.

Even a single phrase can accomplish Concentration: "Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery" — contrast, implied weakness in alternatives, compensating promise — all in six words.

Chapter 13 — Technique 7

Camouflage: Borrowing Conviction for Your Copy

People trust their publication. Some of that trust carries over to the advertising pages. When they lose faith in the publication, advertising effectiveness collapses.

Three Ways to Borrow Believability

1. Format Adaptation

Approximate the format of the medium: their headline type, their body-copy transition, their illustrations, subheads, break-up of space. A single change in format can add 50% to readership. The Wall Street Journal-adapted ad: ugly line drawing instead of photo, old-fashioned sub-headlines, left-placed subheads, bars above/below — ran 19 times monthly with no drop-off.

2. Phraseology/Style Adoption

Adopt the stereotyped phrases of the medium. In newspapers: issue date, city of origin, by-line. ("New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed an audience...") In direct mail: mimic refund checks, government correspondence, confidential reports. In TV: adopt the news format — clipped newscaster phrase, insider tone, documentary feel.

3. Mood Adaptation

Understatement: Simplicity, lack of color words, fewer adjectives, no superlatives, short sentences falling in tone. (VW: "You also won't have any worries about draining the radiator. There is no radiator. Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust.")

Deadly Sincerity: Lean over backwards to point out flaws — so the benefits are believed more deeply. "This is John Horn's first book. He is not an accomplished writer, and it has flaws." THEN: "But there are paragraphs in this book that have never been put down on paper before."

Chapter 14

The Final Touches

Having broken down the ad to analyze the elements — we now have to tie it together once again.

Verification

Statistics, tests, testimonials, authorities, trends, seals of approval. Three rules: keep it short, dramatic, and specific. Proof copy IS selling copy — it must make the prospect hungry to read every word.

Key insight on placement: Proof — like claims — is most effective when the reader unconsciously DEMANDS it and is ready to accept its content. Position increases power. Place your documentation at the point where the reader is SEARCHING for an answer — where it enters as the hero of a drama you've constructed.

Reinforcement

Power Multiplier

In mathematics, 1+1 = 2. In emotional writing, 1+1 can equal 10. Two emotional images joined the right way can have TEN TIMES the impact of either alone.

Avis: "We try harder" (good alone) + "We're only second" (explains why) = combined impact far greater than the logical sum. Creativity can be defined as the ability to combine separate images into a new unity more powerful than the sum of its parts.

Deadly Sincerity works the same way: "This book has flaws" → "But there are paragraphs in this book that have never been put down on paper before" = the frankness makes the praise 10x more believable.

Interweaving

Every sentence should contain not only promise, not only image, not only logic — but as much of all three as possible. Weave together promise, logic, emotion, image. Pack sentences full. Make them blend until it's impossible to pull out the individual threads.

This is not done consciously. You learn the principle, experiment with it, store it in your unconscious — then it becomes one more intuitive tool used at the exact moment needed. The goal: Creativity #2 — a new kind of intuition that is far more skillful than what you had before.

Sensitivity (Ad Architecture)

Every ad has an architecture — the overall pattern of WHEN and WHERE and HOW MANY TIMES you shift from one type of copy to another. The ability to recognize these shift points is what makes your ad stick together or fall apart.

Four structural patterns (from Schwartz's sample ads):

1. Promise-Definition-Documentation-Promise-Catalog: Start with redefinition to establish difference, reinforce with authority, then release the promise catalog.

2. Promise-Interweave (Definition/Proof): Subtlest blending — every word takes on the aura of pure fact. Definition and promise become indistinguishable.

3. Straight Promise-Mechanism Train: Relentless, no documentation at all. Power from sheer momentum and relentlessness of claims.

4. Authority-Promise-Concentration-Promise: For skeptical markets. Long authority reference (12+ paragraphs on the doctor) earns the right to make promises.

"A new structure is a new ad. It brings out new ideas. Gives your promises new sharpness, new flavor, new believability — and even begins to create whole new sections you never dreamed were there in the product before."— Eugene Schwartz

Momentum

Type 1: Momentum Phrases

Direct invitations to read on: "Here's how..." / "Let me explain." / "And yet it's only the beginning." / "Just wait till you try this." / "For example—" Spot them throughout long copy.

Type 2: Incomplete Statements (Teasers)

Make a statement that interests the reader, then purposely leave it incomplete — raising a question he must read on to answer. Three steps: (1) Create interest in a specific point (2) Raise a question about it (3) Imply an answer later in the copy.

"The sixty-second attachment that HAD TO BE BANNED from the great European raceways — because it gave so much added power..."

"AND THEN, NEARLY-WILD STARTED TO BLOOM!"

Mood

Words and rhythms are to the copywriter what line and color are to the painter. Two types of emotion-definers:

1. Image-Sharpeners: "weighted down... solid, blazing rows... so thick you can't even see a leaf" — create visual precision.

2. Emotion-Definers: "precious... blinded... greets" — tell the reader what to FEEL about the images. Their essence is subtlety — if the reader notices them, you've lost him.

Mood-building is deliberate by the copywriter, unnoticed by the reader. It lies underneath the surface, doing its work imperceptibly.

Read your ad out loud. Notice the rhythm. Rich rhythm parallels rich image and builds sensuousness. Machine-gun rhythm (short, choppy, verb-verb-verb) builds momentum and power. No rhythm at all (long, clumsy sentences) can build ingenuousness and reason.
